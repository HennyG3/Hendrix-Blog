Part 01 — Color Temperature

Light has a color, even when it looks white

Every light source gives off a color. We measure it in Kelvin (K), a scale left over from physics: it's the color a theoretical block of metal would glow if you heated it to that temperature. You don't need the physics, just the result — a candle flame sits around 1,900K, a household tungsten bulb around 3,200K, daylight around 5,600K, and an overcast sky can push past 7,000K.

Here's the part that trips people up: low Kelvin numbers look warm and orange, high Kelvin numbers look cool and blue. It's backwards from how we talk about temperature in everyday life — a "cool blue" flame is actually the hottest part of the fire.

White balance is the camera's correction for this. You tell the camera what color of light it's looking at, and it shifts everything to compensate so that white objects actually look white and skin tones look natural. Set white balance to match your light, and the color cast disappears. Mismatch them, and you get a visible tint: too orange if the camera is compensating for cooler light than you actually have, too blue if it's compensating for warmer light than you actually have.

Why this matters on set A window doing daylight (5,600K) next to a lamp doing tungsten (3,200K) in the same shot is a mixed-lighting problem — your camera can only be correctly balanced for one of them at a time. Gaffers fix this physically with colored gels (CTB gel cools tungsten toward daylight, CTO gel warms daylight toward tungsten). You can fix it electronically with white balance, but only for one light source per shot.

Part 02 — Three-Point Lighting

Three lights, three different jobs

Three-point lighting isn't three equally important lights pointed at a face. It's one main light, plus two supporting lights, each doing a specific job. Once you know the job, you know where each light belongs.

Change any one of the three and the whole read of the shot changes — a strong key with no fill reads as dramatic or moody; the same key with a bright fill reads as flat, even, corporate. The ratio between key and fill brightness is really a contrast dial for the whole face.

Light 1

Key

The main light and the one you set exposure by. It decides which side of the face is bright and where the shadow falls. Usually placed to one side of the camera and a little above eye level, roughly 30–45° off-axis.

Light 2

Fill

Softens the shadow the key creates, without erasing it. Placed on the opposite side of the key, and almost always dimmer — if fill matches key, the face goes flat and loses its shape.

Light 3

Back (rim / hair light)

Placed behind the subject, aimed forward and usually kept out of frame. It traces a thin edge of light along hair and shoulders that separates the subject from the background — especially useful when the backdrop is a similar tone to the subject.
